How SpicyCar works

SpicyCar watches a list of cars every day and shows, for one buyer, the cheapest of each anywhere and the cheapest close to home — with the cost of getting a far one home stated, not hidden.

The question

Asking price alone does not tell you what a car costs. A $38,000 car in San Diego and a $45,000 car in Indianapolis are not $7,000 apart for a buyer in Chicago if one of them has to ride a truck for 1,800 miles. SpicyCar shows both numbers side by side, every day — asking as listed, shipping stated — so you can see which cars are sitting, which are being cut, and which just disappeared.

It is built around a buyer — a home zip code, the states they are willing to drive to, and what shipping costs them — and a watchlist of brands, models and trims. The two are configured separately, so a second buyer is an addition rather than a rewrite.

The first buyer is real: someone near Chicago, shopping for a BMW i5 eDrive40 or an i7. The watchlist is every trim of the i4, i5 and i7, so the ones they want can be judged against their siblings — and comparison models from Hyundai, Kia, Audi, Lucid and Chevrolet, so they can be judged against their rivals. The report leads with what is being shopped, in full, and gives every comparison model one line.

Prices

The number everywhere is the asking price, exactly as the dealer listed it. Nothing is adjusted or inferred. Every sort, tile and chart uses it, and miles are shown next to every price so you can weigh them yourself.

Shipping is the one cost SpicyCar adds, and it states it on every car. A car in one of the buyer's states costs nothing to bring home — you drive it. Anything else gets an estimate from the distance between the listing and the buyer's home: $0.65 a mile with a $350 floor, roughly what open-carrier transport costs. If a listing cannot be placed on the map at all, a flat $1,100 is used instead.

asking + shipping        shipping = 0 in-state
                                  = max($350, miles from home × $0.65) otherwise

That sum is never less than asking; it is only ever the listed price plus the truck. Three illustrative cars for a buyer near Chicago whose states are Illinois, Ohio and Indiana:

Car C is still the cheapest by more than $3,000 after the truck — which is the point of stating it. Whether a buyer wants to purchase a car sight-unseen from 1,800 miles is a separate question; the number just makes the trade-off visible.

Spicy picks are the one place miles are priced in — but only to rank, never to change a number you see. A car is eligible under 50,000 miles with no reported accidents and no rental or fleet history. Each eligible car gets a value: asking, plus shipping, plus 30 cents for every mile above 20,000 (or minus, below). It is then compared with the typical value for its own model, so a cheap model does not win just for being cheap; the picks are the cars furthest under typical, and the label says by how much. The price on the card is still the asking price.

Where the data comes from

Listings come from the auto.dev API. Every day at 11:00 UTC a GitHub Action runs the tracker, which fetches each target due that day, states shipping on every listing, and writes three things back into the repository: a CSV with one row per vehicle per day, a Markdown report (also sent by email), and the JSON behind the dashboard.

There is no server and no database. The CSV in the repository is the ledger, and git history is the backup. A same-day re-run replaces that day's rows rather than duplicating them.

Staying inside a free plan

The free plan allows 1,000 calls a month and returns at most 20 listings per call. That shapes the whole design.

  • Two queries per target. One filtered to the buyer's states — the API takes a comma-separated list, so three states cost one call — and one national. Adding a state costs nothing.
  • Depth. The one or two trims a buyer is actually shopping get full depth: two sort orders, two pages each. Everything else gets light: the cheapest 20 and nothing more. The i5 eDrive40 is full; every other target is light.
  • Cadence. Comparison brands are fetched every other day, spread evenly across the cycle in watchlist order, so six brands fit in about 30 calls a day — roughly 900 a month. On a model's off day the dashboard and report show its last fetch, marked "as of".
  • A hard budget. The script prints its plan before it starts — today, the worst day in the next two weeks, the monthly average — and refuses to run if any day would exceed budget_per_day or the average would exceed budget_per_month.

Because each query returns only the cheapest N, the data is a sample of the bottom of the market, not the whole market. That has one consequence worth being explicit about.

A car can leave the data without being sold

If a listing's price rises above the day's cheapest-N cut-off, it simply is not fetched. The dashboard checks a vanished car's last price against that cut-off and labels it priced above today's cut-off rather than gone, and the report leaves those out of its "gone" list. Anything else would be a guess dressed up as a fact.

What counts as local — and why not a radius

The first version scoped by city: a 200-mile radius around each of three Midwest cities, with listings placed by their coordinates. It returned one or two local cars a day, while the same cars showed up in the national results with Midwest addresses.

The cause took a week to find. For listings it cannot geocode, the API returns coordinates of exactly (0, 0) — a point in the Atlantic off West Africa, about 5,900 miles from Indianapolis. Those values are not null, so every emptiness check passed them through, and the distance math confidently placed the cars outside every radius. About 7% of listings were affected, including the two cheapest i5 eDrive40s in Indianapolis.

SpicyCar now scopes by state. A listing is in-state when its own state field is one of the buyer's states — no coordinates involved. Coordinates are used only to estimate shipping, and when they are missing or zero the listing's zip code is geocoded instead, once, with the result cached in the repository.

The rule that came out of it

Use the field that means the thing. State was on every record from the start; deriving it from coordinates was a mistake that looked like a feature.

Reading the dashboard

  • Every model — the front page: one row per model with a photo, cars on the market, in-state count, lowest asking nationwide, lowest asking in-state, the median and a trend line, all under the same filters. Shopping first, then by price.
  • Every model over time — one line per model, lowest asking price by day. The model being shopped is in colour; the rest are context, each named at its line's end.
  • Spicy picks — four cars, on the front page across every model and on each model within it: photo, asking price, the shipping line, and how far under typical the car sits. Recomputed as you filter.
  • Know the model — on every model page: the good, the bad, and what to check before you go and look. Written by hand and kept in the config, so it can be corrected as we learn.
  • Listings — one row per VIN with photo, asking, miles, the shipping estimate, asking plus shipping, history flags (rental and fleet cars are marked), distance from home, days on market, and a price sparkline. Filter by trim, state, year and miles, hide accidents, hide rentals and fleet; sort by asking, asking plus shipping, miles, biggest drop, longest on market, or newest.
  • Gone from the market — with the out-of-window flag described above.

The signals worth acting on: days on market past 30 usually means a dealer who will negotiate. Price cuts show how many times and how far. new marks cars first seen today. 1-owner, no accidents and cpo are the history flags worth filtering on; ex-lease usually means one careful owner and a full service record.

What it does not know

  • It samples the cheapest end of the market. A car that never sat in the bottom 20–80 for its trim never appears.
  • Shipping is an estimate. Get a quote before believing it to the dollar.
  • Miles are shown, not priced. A 5,000-mile car and a 50,000-mile car at the same asking price sort together; the miles column is there so you can tell them apart.
  • State is a proxy for "drivable." Eastern Ohio is 350 miles from Chicago; Benton Harbor, Michigan is 90 — and pays shipping.
  • One data source. Private-party sales, and dealers not in the feed, are invisible.
  • Taxes, dealer fees, and the plane ticket to go and look are not included.

Roadmap

  • Drill below state: county or metro.
  • Distance-based "drivable" instead of state lines.
  • A second buyer profile — the config is already shaped for it.
  • More brands on the watchlist.
