How SpicyCar works

SpicyCar watches a list of cars every day and shows, for one buyer, the cheapest of each anywhere and the cheapest close to home — with the cost of getting a far one home stated, not hidden.

The question

Asking price alone does not tell you what a car costs. A $38,000 car in San Diego and a $45,000 car in Indianapolis are not $7,000 apart for a buyer in Chicago if one of them has to ride a truck for 1,800 miles. SpicyCar shows both numbers side by side, every day — asking as listed, shipping stated — so you can see which cars are sitting, which are being cut, and which just disappeared.

It is built around a buyer — a home anchor, the states they are willing to drive to, and what shipping costs them — and a watchlist of brands, models and trims. The two are configured separately, so a second buyer is an addition rather than a rewrite.

The first buyer is real: someone near Chicago, deciding between a BMW i5 eDrive40 and an i7 eDrive50. The watchlist is the i5 and i7 in depth — the i5 also carrying a nationwide watch on certified cars under 30,000 miles, where the promo financing rate is most of the payment (the i7's was written and stood down before it ever ran: on miles.asc the forty lowest-mileage i7s in the country are all uncertified 2026 delivery stock, so no certified car falls inside the window) — their sibling the iX for comparison, and rivals from Hyundai, Kia, Audi and Lucid, so the two they are choosing between can be judged against each other and against the market. The report leads with what is being shopped, in full, and gives every comparison model one line.

Prices

The number everywhere is the asking price, exactly as the dealer listed it. Nothing is adjusted or inferred. Every sort, tile and chart uses it, and miles are shown next to every price so you can weigh them yourself.

Shipping is the one cost SpicyCar adds, and it states it on every car. A drivable car costs nothing to bring home — you drive it. Anything else gets an estimate in two steps. First the straight-line distance from the listing to the buyer's anchor point is inflated by 1.18 into road miles, because a truck follows interstates around lakes and terrain and a great-circle line does not. Then those road miles are priced through distance bands, marginally — like tax brackets: $1.20 a mile for the first 500, $0.70 to 1,000, $0.45 to 1,500 and $0.30 beyond, with a $350 floor. Each band applies only to the miles inside it, so a longer haul always costs more in total while costing less per mile — which is how transport actually prices, since the carrier's fixed costs are the same for 200 miles as for 2,000. If a listing cannot be placed on the map at all, a flat $1,100 is used instead.

Those band rates are published typical open-carrier ranges, not quotes anyone obtained. The shape of the model is defensible; its constants are not yet. Every shipping figure on this site is an estimate until real quotes calibrate it, and the site says so rather than presenting a guess as research.

asking + shipping   shipping = 0 if drivable, else max($350, banded(road))

                    road = straight-line miles from home x 1.18

                    banded(m) = 1.20/mi on the first        500 road miles
                              + 0.70/mi on the next         500  (to 1,000)
                              + 0.45/mi on the next         500  (to 1,500)
                              + 0.30/mi on everything beyond

That sum is never less than asking; it is only ever the listed price plus the truck. Three illustrative cars for a buyer near Chicago whose states are Illinois, Ohio, Indiana and Wisconsin:

Car C is still the cheapest by more than $2,600 after the truck — which is the point of stating it. Whether a buyer wants to purchase a car sight-unseen from 1,800 miles is a separate question; the number just makes the trade-off visible.

Spicy picks are the one place miles are priced in — but only to rank, never to change a number you see. A car is eligible under 50,000 miles with no reported accidents and no rental or fleet history. Each eligible car gets a value: asking, plus shipping, plus 30 cents for every mile above 20,000 (or minus, below). It is then compared with the typical value for its own cohort — its own trim and model year where six or more eligible cars share them, else the model year, else the whole model — so a cheap model does not win just for being cheap. The picks come in two lists under that one rule — the best values in your states, and the best values worth the ship from everywhere else — and only cars genuinely under typical qualify, so a thin drivable market never promotes an ordinary car. Genuinely is arithmetic, not taste: a median of nine cars has a 95% interval of its own, and a car is under typical only when its value sits below that interval. On today's sheet a 2025 eDrive40 seven percent under the median of nine sits on that interval's low edge, so the page cannot tell the car from typical and says nothing — no note, no chip, no pick — rather than reading noise as a discount; whether a margin clears the edge depends on how spread the cohort is, never on its size alone. A margin that rounds to nothing is not called under either, because “0% under typical” is a number with no content. Six cars make a cohort, because five is the largest sample with no such interval at all; and at six, seven or eight the interval is the whole sample, so no car in a cohort that small is ever called under or over. And the cohort has to be comparable, which the interval cannot ask: it says whether a median is stable, not whether the cars behind it are the same kind of car. Four 2022 iX xDrive50s read “26–40% under typical for a BMW iX, n=21” against a pool of ten M60s and eleven xDrive50s — the M60 is a different car at a different price, so the percentage was pricing the pool's trim mix. So a cohort speaks only when every car in it wears the scored car's trim: automatic when the cohort is the car's own trim and model year, an accident when it is the year or the whole model. Where it is not, the page says too few comparable listings to say and prints no percentage — not on the row, not on a chip, not as a pick, and not as a place in the best-value order either, since ranking a car first on a number the page will not print is making the claim with position instead of words. The label says how far under typical each pick sits; the price on the card is still the asking price.

Where the data comes from

Listings come from the auto.dev API. Every day at 11:00 UTC a GitHub Action runs the tracker, which fetches each target due that day, states shipping on every listing, and writes three things back into the repository: a CSV with one row per vehicle per day, a Markdown report — the day's committed record, not a message; the email path is switched off on purpose — and the JSON behind the dashboard.

There is no server and no database. The CSV in the repository is the ledger, and git history is the backup. A same-day re-run replaces that day's rows rather than duplicating them.

Staying inside a free plan

The free plan allows 1,000 calls a month and returns at most 20 listings per call. That shapes the whole design.

  • Two queries per target. One filtered to the buyer's states plus the search_states worth watching from beyond — the API takes a comma-separated list, so eight states cost one call — and one national. Adding a state costs nothing. The exception is the nationwide CPO watches: they ask only the national question, sorted by lowest mileage, because the states are a subset of the same answer and the saved call is what makes a coast-to-coast watch affordable.
  • Depth. The two targets being shopped get full depth: two sort orders at two pages each, plus one newest-first page per query — the price and miles sorts sample the settled bottom of the market, and a fresh, well-priced car can sell before it ever ranks there. New cars lead the report as New today. Everything else gets light: the cheapest 20 and nothing more.
  • Cadence. BMW siblings are fetched every other day and rival brands every third day, spread evenly across the cycle in watchlist order — about 29 calls a day, roughly 884 a month, with the worst day in any fortnight at 32 against a hard cap of 40. Trims of one model share a fetch day, but only with the trims that run at the same rate: a third-day trim takes a slot in the third-day rotation, not its model’s second-day one. On a model's off day the dashboard and report show its last fetch, dated to that day — "data through" on the dashboard, "as of" in the report.
  • A hard budget. The script prints its plan before it starts — today, the worst day in the next two weeks, the monthly average — and refuses to run if any day would exceed budget_per_day or the average would exceed budget_per_month.

Because each query returns only the cheapest N, the data is a sample of the bottom of the market, not the whole market. That has one consequence worth being explicit about.

A car can leave the data without being sold

If a listing's price rises above the day's cheapest-N cut-off, it simply is not fetched. The dashboard checks a vanished car's last price against that cut-off and labels it beyond that day's fetch cut-off rather than gone, and the report leaves those out of its "gone" list. Anything else would be a guess dressed up as a fact. The CPO watches sort by mileage instead of price, so their cut-off lives on the same axis: a vanished car is judged against the deepest mileage that day's fetch reached, not against a price that never gated anything.

And a departure from one query is not a departure from the market. A certified watch's market is “certified cars under the mileage cap”, so a car that loses its badge leaves that watch for real — but it is still for sale, and the page said “GONE — the listing ended” over a car sitting live in its own listings table two cards down. Such a row now names what happened instead: left the CPO under 30k mi — the same VIN is listed as xDrive40 at $51,476, not certified. It names the other record as its own, because that is what it is: the snapshot holds both on the same day, at different prices and sometimes under different dealer names, so the departed listing was never cut to the surviving one. And it leads with the certification, because the promo is certified-only — a VIN that survives uncertified is a car whose payment just moved by about $92 a month, and “still listed” on its own would report that as good news. Only against a record from the day the model was last fetched: trims run different cadences, so “live on the sheet” can mean a sighting as old as the departure itself. It keeps its place in the departures card, because for a buyer chasing the certified rate that is the news; what it leaves is every count and every price that means left the market — the movement tile's “N gone”, the exit price and its interval, the typical days a listing ran, and the report's “Gone since” block, which now heads a “Left this watch, still listed” block instead. The departures card says how many were set aside, the way it already says how many were beyond a fetch cut-off.

What counts as drivable — your states, nothing else

The first version scoped by city: a 200-mile radius around each of three Midwest cities, with listings placed by their coordinates. It returned one or two local cars a day, while the same cars showed up in the national results with Midwest addresses.

The cause took a week to find. For listings it cannot geocode, the API returns coordinates of exactly (0, 0) — a point in the Atlantic off West Africa, about 5,900 miles from Indianapolis. Those values are not null, so every emptiness check passed them through, and the distance math confidently placed the cars outside every radius. About 7% of listings were affected, including the two cheapest i5 eDrive40s in Indianapolis.

SpicyCar checks state, and only state: a listing is drivable when its own state field is one of the buyer's states. No coordinates are involved, so a car the API could not place on the map still lands in the right bucket — and the line sits exactly where the buyer drew it. There used to be a five-hour drive radius layered on top; it was removed on purpose. A radius sounds generous but it is a claim about roads that straight-line miles cannot honestly make, and it blurred the one question this bucket answers: will I go get it myself? States are the buyer's own answer to that. Benton Harbor, Michigan — 90 miles from Chicago — now pays the $350 shipping floor, because Michigan is not on this buyer's list; add Michigan to buyer.states and every Michigan car flips to drivable on the next run.

Which leaves one case the rule does not cover, and the page used to answer it wrongly. Three live cars — an i5 at $47,990, an iX at $33,990, a Lucid Air at $37,990, eight VINs over the whole record — come back from the API with no state at all. They are not drivable, because drivable means the state is one of the buyer's and there is no state to check. They are not beyond the buyer's states either, because nothing here knows where they are. Every count that opposed those two buckets swept them silently into the second, and the decision tile printed “beyond your states” beside a location cell showing a dash. They keep the flat shipping estimate — a car that cannot be confirmed drivable has to be priced as though it ships, which is the conservative direction — and they lose the claim about where they are: the tiles count them apart as “1 with no state”, the row and the chip say “state not given”, and the daily record carries the same split. Not “no location”, which the map already uses for cars with no coordinates: a different set, and scope here is state membership and nothing else.

The rule that came out of it

Use the field that means the thing. State was on every record from the start; deriving it from coordinates was a mistake that looked like a feature. A radius did return briefly — four days, layered on top of the states — before being removed for good: state alone decides now, so the line is the buyer's own, and a car the map cannot place still lands in the right bucket.

One more honesty note, about privacy this time: every distance measures from a public anchor — downtown Chicago, set as coordinates in the config — never from anyone's home. A published distance is an exact constraint, and hundreds of dealer coordinates plus a distance each would pin a private origin down to a city block no matter how the numbers were rounded. Anchoring to a public point makes the whole question moot. Distances are also rounded to 25 miles, because they are estimates for judging a drive, not measurements.

Reading the dashboard

In the order the page presents them — what you can buy first, the evidence behind it next, the reference material last.

  • The decision — the top of the watchlist, and the only card that knows which cars this is actually about: one tile per model named in buyer.shopping, each holding the cheapest car of its watched trims. Cheapest all in — asking plus shipping plus tax and paperwork — because a car in California and a car in Ohio are not comparable on the sticker when one of them owes a hauler $1,336, and because the buyer is choosing between totals. That total is an estimate and the tile says so on its own label. Under it: what the total is made of, the payment at that car's own rate, the car itself, where it is, the cheapest one you could drive to instead and what that costs — priced the way the winner is, at its own rate over its own term, with the gap stated in cash by the end of each loan rather than on the sticker, because a certified car on a promo can be dearer all in and cheaper to own — and what driving to one has cost over the record, not only today: the drivable floor against the floor on each fetch day with two or more drivable cars (one car is a price, not a premium), the range over those days with the pool size beside it, since “$349–$8,495 over 14 fetch days, on 2–3 drivable cars” is a different claim from the same range over thirty, and only once seven such days exist — and how far the car sits from typical for its own trim and model year — in both directions, because a cheapest car that is also above typical is exactly what you would want to know first, and “about typical” when it sits inside its cohort's own interval, because a number the data cannot defend is not printed here either. Cars your own rules exclude — over the mileage cap, an accident on record, a rental past — are not shown and not hidden either: the tile counts them and names the rules. Under the car, the same number day by day: a sparkline of the cheapest rule-fit car, all in, on each day the shopped trims were fetched — never a day carried forward, never a day the fit pool held fewer than five cars, nothing under three such days — with a caption saying how many different cars held that floor, how long the current one has, and what the first day's figure was; and, for today, how far behind the next car sits and how many other cars are within $1,000 of the floor — a floor with nothing behind it is one listing's whim, a floor with two cars inside $1,000 is a price. One line under the two tiles states the gap between them, in dollars and per month, and — over the days both were fetched, five at least — its range, its first and last value, and how much of the change was each side's own floor moving, because "the premium fell $5,462" reads as the i7 getting cheaper when $4,481 of it was the i5's floor rising. Floors only, never a median gap: a median moves when coverage moves. Then what the money buys, in the sheet's own columns for those two cars and no others: model year, miles, owners, certification, days listed — “1 model year older with 8,000 more miles; 3 owners against 1; certified, the other not” — with no adjective and no verdict, because whether that is worth the premium is the buyer's call, not the page's. A column absent on either side is left out, and an owner count of 0 counts as absent: it is the API's “not reported”, not a car nobody has owned. Then the premium as a trade rather than a subtraction: what the dearer floor's total buys among the cheaper model's own rule-fit cars — “buys 42 of the 48 BMW i5s that fit your rules — the newest a 2025 with 5,770 miles, the lowest-mileage a 2024 with 5,171; 3 drivable, 10 certified (6 at a seller named BMW)” — and what the dearer car would have to ask to cost the same per month, the loan run backwards at that car's own rate over its own term, with shipping inside the loan only where the fee block says it is. Asking rather than all in, because asking is the number a buyer can act on at a dealer; and no payment line at all when the two cars finance over different terms, because “the same a month” across two terms compares two loans, not two cars. Each tile's link opens the car, not just the model: it lands on the car's own row in the listings — opening the list out when the row sits past the first thirty — and focuses it, so the keyboard and the eyes arrive together. On a phone each tile keeps its headline, its typical stand and its link on the first screen and folds the evidence — the floor day by day, what stands behind it, the drivable alternative, what driving has cost — behind one disclosure, the way the nationwide picks fold; the gap line keeps its first sentence and folds the record behind it. A desktop has the room and shows it all. It is the front page only; a model page is already about one model.
  • Today at a glance — four tiles: the lowest asking price you can drive to, the lowest anywhere, how many cars are on the market, and what moved since the last snapshot — where “new” means new to the tracker, and the tile says how many of its new cars carried a listing date a fortnight or more before it first saw them, because a car that sat just outside a forty-car fetch window until something ahead of it left is reach, not an arrival; the comparison's movement row says the same. They follow the filters and say "(filtered)" when they are narrowed, so a floor under a filter is never quoted as the nation's. On the watchlist a tile led by a model on a slower cadence says "as of Sep 2" beside the car — only when that model's last fetch is older than the masthead's date, never as a count of days. On a model page the lowest-asking tile's ▲▼ says why the floor moved, from the two floor cars: the same car cut or raised, another car arriving below the floor or cut below it, or the old floor car leaving — "left the market" only when the record confirmed the departure, "stopped being seen" otherwise. Every floor move on this record so far was a car arriving or leaving, and an arrow alone read as the market moving. When the cars in view are two markets — dealer stock under 100 miles, priced near sticker, beside used cars at half that — the market tile says so on its face, with a count and a median for each side, because one median across both describes neither.
  • What the promo is worth — when a promotional finance rate is live, a strip that prices it on the cheapest car that can actually have it: the payment now and the payment the day after the offer ends, the interest saved over the whole term, and what an ordinary-rate car would have to ask to match that payment. The rate in the heading is the rate the car in the figures is financed at, never the first live offer on the sheet. Certified cars listed by a seller whose name does not say BMW are excluded from the figures and counted underneath, because a manufacturer's certified rate is written at a manufacturer's store.
  • Since your visit — the page remembers, in your browser, the newest data day you have seen, and when you come back to newer data the front page opens with one sentence on what changed since: each shopped model's floor then and now (the same fetch-day ledger the tiles draw), how many cars were first seen since, how many ask less than they did that day — today's price under that day's price, because a cut that bounced back is not a cut you can act on — and the departures, worded by what the sheet knows: gone only for the rows the tracker is exact about, stopped being seen for the rest, which may simply have fallen outside a fetch window. It says nothing on a first visit, nothing when your remembered day sits before the record it can count against, and never over a notice that already has something to say. Nothing about you leaves the browser: the remembered day is a date in localStorage and the counts are made from the same public sheet every visit reads.
  • Filters — a bar directly under the tiles that stays pinned to the top as you scroll, so the controls are always to hand and never below the things they change. It names what it is doing ("showing 232 of 629 cars · IL"); on a phone it collapses to a single row that expands on tap. Three of the controls are multi-selects that work identically — press any mix, nothing pressed means all of them: Where (your states, plus "beyond"), Models on the watchlist, and Trim on a model page, each chip carrying the number of cars behind it. They narrow the whole page — tiles, picks, tables, chart, map. Beside them a VIN field — the full seventeen characters off a windshield, or the last six you remember from it — which opens that car's model with the car itself at the top, in the notice slot, and its row landed on; six characters that fit two cars say so and open nothing, five are refused (the last four are a plate, not a car), and a VIN nobody has seen goes through the same dead-link notice as a model nobody tracks, with the address bar cleaned so a bad link is not re-shared. A typed VIN and a shared ?vin= link are one path. Then a Budget, measured either all in (the out-the-door total, or the landed price where the sheet has no fee block) or a month (each car at its own rate over its own term, so a certified car on a promo can fit a budget a cheaper car misses). It is a filter like the others, remembered between visits like the term and the down payment, and a car the sheet cannot price for the chosen unit does not slip through it — nothing here may claim a car fits a budget it was never measured against. When a budget empties one of the models you are shopping, the decision panel says so in those words: "79 would fit your rules — none under $50,000 all in". Where is remembered between visits; the models and trims you picked go in the address bar instead, because they are what the page is about rather than how you like to read it.
  • Side by side — press two models, or two trims of one model, and the page stops being a survey and becomes a comparison: a column each, with the lowest drivable price, the lowest anywhere, the cheapest landed, the median, the count, the median miles, the typical days on market (the list sentence's own figure for the same cars: twelve dated cars or nothing, truncated, with its denominator, and each market's own days where dealer stock sits beside used cars), the best value under typical, what moved since the last snapshot and — where any column holds dealer stock — how many of its cars are under 100 miles, with the stock's median beside the used cars' own, running down the side. At most one row marks a winner — best value vs typical — and that is deliberate: a price is not a score, and marking the cheapest column "best" would only be saying that the smaller car costs less. Even that row goes unmarked unless every column's best car had enough siblings of its own trim and model year to be judged against them; where it does not, "typical" quietly means something different in each column and the numbers are left to speak for themselves. Comparing models also pools every one of their cars into a single ranked table, so "the cheapest of either, that I can drive to" is one sort away. The chart follows: two things picked draws two lines, never one merged one, because each has its own floor and a median across them would describe no car on the market. Clearing the filters clears the filters — the comparison you built stays.
  • Your shortlist, side by side — the one card that holds your opinion rather than the market's. Every car on the page carries a star with four states rather than two: none, shortlisted, called, ruled out. Star two and a table appears with a column each, cheapest first — asking, out the door, per month at that car's own rate, miles, value against its own cohort, where it is, what its price has done, and a sparkline. A ruled-out car leaves the comparison and keeps its mark, because "I looked at this one and said no" is the fact worth keeping; a starred car that has since left the market stays in the table wearing gone. Each row names its own winner in its own words — lowest asking, fewest miles — and none of them says "best value": that belongs to the one row measured against a cohort, and two cars scored against two different cohorts are not comparable on the percentage, so that row marks no winner at all. It lives in your browser, not in the config: a config edit needs a commit and a decision does not.
  • Spicy picks — two lists, on the front page across every model and on each model within it: the best values in your states, and the best worth shipping from anywhere else. Photo (press it to open the listing), asking price, the shipping line, and how far under typical the car sits — a strong saving is green, a slim one grey, so a 2% pick never wears the same badge as a 30% one. Each card says whether the model is one you are shopping or a comparison, and the shopped ones lead. On a phone the nationwide list folds behind its own headline. Recomputed as you filter.
  • Listings — one row per VIN with photo, asking, miles, the shipping estimate, asking plus shipping, history flags (rental and fleet cars are marked), distance from home, days on market, how many times the tracker has seen the car and over how many days ("seen 6 of 12 days" — sightings, never elapsed days, because a trim fetched every second day must not read as tracked for twice as long), and a price sparkline. Filter by trim (press one, or several to compare them), where, year and miles, hide accidents, hide rentals and fleet, show only certified (CPO) cars — the ones the promo financing applies to — or hide delivery-mileage stock (cars under 100 miles: demos, loaners and unregistered stock that the used-listings feed serves beside the used cars, at a different price; miles, not model year, because a 2026 with 5,000 miles on it is a used car); sort by drivable-first (the default), newest, asking, asking plus shipping, out the door (all in), monthly payment, best value vs typical, miles, biggest drop, cut at the latest snapshot, or longest on market. The table shows the top thirty under the current sort; one press shows them all. On a phone each car becomes a stacked card instead of a table; on a desktop the vehicle column stays pinned while the rest scrolls, and the landed total sits beside the shipping estimate rather than in a column of its own. Under the count a market line describes the cars in view — the trim and filters you picked, not the whole model, so an eDrive50 is never told how fast xDrive60s left — each figure with its own denominator: how long the typical car has been listed (and how many carried a listing date — and, where dealer stock sits beside used cars with twelve dated on each side, each market's own figure, because 41 stock cars at 72 days beside 30 used at 17 blend to a 39 no car sits at), how many cars ask less than when first seen (with the median drop, and how many "cuts" were put back — a step that bounced back on the next fetch is not a price a buyer can act on), the share cut while tracked (over the cars it counts, with the median cut over the number of cuts it counts — the two figures count different things, cars and downward steps, and one clause used to name only one of them; a median of $600 over 139 steps cannot be propped up by a $1 move, which is the honest answer to a small move without inventing a threshold the record cannot defend) — with the cars seen at two prices set aside and counted apart: a VIN that surfaces at two fixed prices in turn, each seen at least twice with a step each way, is the storefront sawtooth of a group listing one car through two fronts, and it read as four cuts and four restorations; its row now says "seen at $54,999 and $55,849", its down days are not cuts on the movement tile, and it is in none of the cut figures — and how long the listings that ended had run, where at least twelve of this scope's own did.
  • Price against miles — on every model page: one dot per car, coloured by trim where the model has more than one (across the watchlist colour is the brand; inside one model the brand is a constant and carries nothing, so the trim takes the channel), filled when drivable, hollow when it pays shipping, the spicy picks ringed, and a dashed typical-value line per model year — a dot below its year's line asks less than a typical car of that year, miles allowed for. Hover a line or press its year chip to light up that year's cars; hover a dot for the car, click to open the listing.
  • The market over time — one line per model: colour is the brand, the dash pattern is the model within it, and the models being shopped are drawn heavier. The legend is the control — click a chip to hide or show a model, hover one to pick its line out of the crowd — and the crosshair reads out every model's price for any day. The 30d / 90d / All chips set the time window (remembered between visits), and the price scale fits the window, so recent movement stays readable as months of history pile up. On a model page with two or more trims picked it draws one line per trim on one scale, each from that trim's own record. Like everything else on the page, the lines follow the filters: pick Illinois and each line becomes that model's lowest asking price in Illinois each day, rebuilt from the price history every car carries — cars that have since left the market included, so a day is not made to look more expensive than it was by the cheap car that sold on it.
  • The market on the map — on the front page and on every model page: each car at its own coordinates on a US map, coloured by brand — filled dots are drivable, hollow ones pay shipping. The view follows the Where chips: pick a state and the map zooms to it; with nothing picked it frames the cars themselves, so a regional market fills the frame while a coast-to-coast one reads as the whole country. Pinch or Ctrl/⌘‑scroll to zoom further (plain scrolling keeps scrolling the page), drag with a mouse or two fingers to pan, and the ⤢ button resets. Hover a dot for the car — photo included; on a phone the first tap previews and a second tap opens. On a model page a click opens the listing, on the front page it opens the model. Spicy picks are ringed in the accent, and shortlisted cars are drawn in it outright — the cars that matter are findable at a glance. Zoom in and quiet state labels fade in, so a tight frame still tells you where you are; hover the home dot for what distances measure from.
  • Know the model — on every model page: the good, the bad, and what to check before you go and look. Written by hand and kept in the config, so it can be corrected as we learn.
  • Missing from the latest snapshot — the twelve most recent vehicles missing from it, confirmed departures first, with the out-of-window flag described above; one press shows the full list. Stacked cards on a phone, like the listings.

The daily REPORT.md is the same claims in a file, and it is held to the same rule as the page: a figure carries the denominator that makes it true, or it is not printed. The signals worth acting on: days on market past 30 usually means a dealer who will negotiate. Price cuts show how many times and how far. new marks cars first seen today. 1-owner, no accidents and cpo are the history flags worth filtering on; ex-lease usually means one careful owner and a full service record.

What it does not know

  • It samples the cheapest end of the market, plus — for the shopped trims — the newest listings each day. A comparison-model car that never sat in the bottom of its trim's price order never appears.
  • Shipping is an estimate. Get a quote before believing it to the dollar.
  • Miles are shown, not priced. A 5,000-mile car and a 50,000-mile car at the same asking price sort together; the miles column is there so you can tell them apart.
  • Drivable is the state on the listing, not the miles. A car five miles across a state line counts as shipped, and the far corner of your own state counts as drivable — the line sits exactly where you drew it, which is the point, but it is a line, not a judgement. Shipping distances are rounded to 25 miles on purpose.
  • One data source. Private-party sales, and dealers not in the feed, are invisible.
  • Out-the-door and monthly figures DO include tax and paperwork — Illinois charges the buyer's home rate wherever the car is bought, so buyer.fees adds it to every car, near or far — but nobody has verified those rates: buyer.fees.checked is null and the page says "estimate" wherever they appear. The plane ticket to go and look is not included.
  • Days on market come from the API's createdAt, which is when the RECORD was made. A bulk index load stamps thousands of cars with one date — this feed's is 2026-08-09, carried by a third of every snapshot across 25 states and 85 dealers — and a date like that is detected and the age withheld rather than published as a listing age. Those cars show no "days listed" at all, which is the honest answer.

Roadmap

  • Drill below state: county or metro.
  • Compare two cars at once, not one at a time. Shipped — press two model or two trim chips; see Side by side.
  • Distance-based "drivable" instead of state lines. Tried, then removed on purpose — state lines are the buyer's own answer to "will I go get it?", and a radius was a road claim straight-line miles could not honestly make.
  • A second buyer profile — the config is already shaped for it.
  • Alerts worth waking for: email only when a new spicy pick appears or a watched VIN is cut.
